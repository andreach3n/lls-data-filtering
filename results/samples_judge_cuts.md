````text
Viruses and bacteria are both microscopic organisms that can infect host organisms, but they differ significantly in structure, reproduction, and impact on the host.

### Viruses
Viruses are acellular, meaning they don't have a cell structure on their own. They are essentially biologically active molecules such as DNA or RNA surrounded by a protein coat. This protein shell, known as a capsid, can be enveloped by a lipid membrane, but the virus itself is not considered alive because it cannot carry out life processes without a host cell.

Key characteristics of viruses
1. Reproduction: Viruses reproduce by entering a host cell and commandeering its machinery to make more viruses. This process, called lysis, can destroy the host cell.
2. Dependency: Viruses are obligate intracellular parasites, meaning they cannot reproduce or survive outside a host cell.
3. Size: Viruses are typically much smaller than bacteria. 

### Bacteria
Bacteria are single-celled organisms that are prokaryotic, meaning they don't have a nucleus. They are complex, capable of producing chemical energy from organic substrates, and have a much more pronounced metabolism. Bacteria maintain themselves using simple structures like flagella for movement, and they can exist as either parasites or as symbiotic organisms living in partnership with other organisms.

Key characteristics of bacteria
1. Reproduction: Bacteria reproduce through binary fission, a process where one cell divides into two genetically identical cells; unlike viruses, bacteria don't rely on host cells for reproduction.
2. Autonomy: They are self-sustaining and can live independently without a host cell.
3. Size: Bacteria are typically larger than viruses, with diameters ranging from 0.5 to 5.0 micrometers.

In summary, while both viruses and bacteria can cause diseases, they are very different at the cellular level, in how they reproduce and interact with their environment. Bacteria tend to be more damaging to humans in ways that viruses aren't because of their direct impact on our body's health, their capability to form colonies that cause infections, diseases, and sometimes, harm.
````

## What does 'statistically significant' actually mean?

`controls_eosfix|no_removal|general|00110` — judge note: Complete answer about statistical significance, ends with follow-up offer — kept 377 of 388 words

````text
---- JUDGE: answer starts ----
"Statistically significant" is a term that arises in hypothesis testing, not in the *essence* of data itself, but how we interpret results. Here's what it actually means:

### 1. **Definition**:  
A result is *statistically significant* if the observed effect (e.g., difference in means, correlation) is unlikely to occur by random chance alone, assuming a null hypothesis (no real effect).  

### 2. **How we judge it**:  
- **Null hypothesis (H₀)**: You assume no effect exists (e.g., "Thumb length is unrelated to eye color").  
- **Alternative hypothesis (H₁)**: You believe an effect exists.  
- We calculate a **p-value**, the probability of observing the data (or more extreme) *if H₀ were true*.  
- If **p < threshold** (often **0.05**), we reject H₀—saying the result is "statistically significant."  

### 3. **Key Clarifications**:  
- **p < 0.05 does *not* guarantee truth**: It means the result is *unlikely* under H₀, not impossible. A p = 0.049 doesn’t prove H₁; it could be a fluke.  
- **Effect size matters**: A tiny effect can be "significant" with a massive sample. For example:  
  - A correlation of **r = 0.000001** (p < 0.05) might look significant with 1 million observations—but the effect is irrelevant.  
- **>0.05 ≠ proof of no effect**: It simply means evidence favors H₀.  
- **Statistical significance ≠ Practical importance**: A huge sample might detect a minuscule effect as "significant."  

### 4. **Why it’s fragile**  
- p-values depend on sample size. Small samples inflate risk of false positives.  
- **p-hacking**: Manipulating data (e.g., selective variable selection) can falsely make results "significant."  
- Scientific rigor demands domain knowledge—stats cannot confirm truth alone.  

### 5. **What it *doesn’t* mean**:  
✅ ❌ It doesn’t prove your idea.  
✅ ❌ It doesn’t measure truth.  
✅ ❌ It doesn’t imply the effect is "big."  
✅ ❌ It doesn’t rule out chance (even a single study can be wrong).  

### 6. **Practical Tip**  
When reporting results:  
- Always include effect size (e.g., *r = 0.3*, *p = 0.01*).  
- Avoid over-emphasizing "significant."  
- Ask: Is this result too small, too big, or non-existent?  

**Example**:  
- If a thumb-size study finds *p = 0.04*, it’s "statistically significant" by convention, but one more year of data might shatter it.  
- Real-world decisions (e.g., drug trials) depend on interpretation, not just p-values.
````

````text
- Structural instability (buckling, settling).  
- Increased maintenance or even collapse risks.
````
